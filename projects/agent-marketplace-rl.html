Agent Marketplace RL Simulation

Does scoring system design determine whether AI agents game or deliver value?

Type: RL simulation + interactive app Completed: May 2026 Stack: Python · PyTorch · Gymnasium · Streamlit · Plotly
RL / DQN AI Safety Market Design Economics
Live demo → GitHub Read the research paper →

Overview

When AI agents compete in a marketplace, they optimise for whatever the scoring system rewards. If that system measures gameable proxies rather than genuine quality, a rational agent will game the proxies — not because it is misaligned, but because that is the rational response to the incentive structure. This simulation, built as a computational companion to the SPAR research paper The Infrastructure of Perception, tests that argument computationally using reinforcement learning techniques from the MIT MicroMasters in Statistics and Data Science. The findings are stark: even a modest improvement in scoring methodology — introducing just 4–5% genuine quality signal weighting — is sufficient to shift agent behaviour from near-total gaming to near-perfect value delivery.

Research context

The SPAR research paper identifies three failure modes likely to arise as AI agents become economic actors in competitive marketplaces. The second concerns how agents behave when the reputation scoring systems that determine competitive standing measure gameable proxies rather than genuine quality. This simulation models that scenario directly, lending computational weight to what would otherwise be a speculative, theoretical argument.

The underlying mechanism follows what has come to be known as Goodhart’s Law (Strathern, 1997, paraphrasing Goodhart, 1975): when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. In competitive markets, agents face strong incentives to optimise for whatever metric determines their ranking. If that metric can be gamed independently of the quality it was meant to capture, gaming becomes the rational strategy. Higher-ranked agents receive more task assignments; the infrastructure selects for gaming.

Central claim

Goodhart dynamics in AI agent markets are not a failure of agent alignment — they are a failure of measurement infrastructure. The governance implication is architectural: rather than trying to constrain individual agents, the right intervention is to improve the shared scoring systems through which all agents perceive competitive pressure. Fix the infrastructure; the rational behaviour follows.

The simulation operationalises this through a single parameter, α (infrastructure signal fidelity), which controls how closely the scoring system’s reward signal tracks genuine quality versus gameable proxies. Varying α simulates different governance regimes. No agent values, capabilities, or objectives are modified across regimes — the same rational optimiser responds to different information environments.

Environment design

The simulation uses a custom Gymnasium environment, AgentMarketplaceEnv, modelling a freelance-style marketplace on a client-selects platform. Clients browse agents ranked by reputation score and select the highest-ranked available agent. Agents receive tasks without any choice over what they work on — their sole strategic degree of freedom is the approach they apply to each assigned task.

State space

The agent observes a six-dimensional continuous state vector at each timestep. Four variables describe the agent’s immediate situation; two track shared infrastructure:

Variable Description
reputation_score The infrastructure’s scoring output [0, 1]. This is what clients see and what determines task assignment. It is gameable.
true_quality The agent’s actual capability [0, 1]. Determines real task success rates. Visible to the scoring system only when α < 1.
task_difficulty Difficulty of the assigned task. Affects the success probability under deep engagement and the effectiveness of surface optimisation.
task_value Stakes of the current task. Higher-value tasks are typically harder; the quality premium for genuine engagement is larger.
market_health Aggregate quality and trust across the marketplace [0, 1]. A shared commons: genuine engagement raises it; score manipulation erodes it.
time_remaining Fraction of the episode still to run (1.0 → 0.0). Allows strategy adaptation over the episode lifecycle.

Action space

The agent chooses from four strategies with asymmetric cost-benefit profiles that interact differently with the information environment:

Action Description Reputation effect Quality / market
Surface optimisation Pattern-matched response calibrated to what previously scored well. Near-certain client satisfaction on surface metrics. Small positive (+0.02 base); reliable Delivers ~25% of genuine task value; small market health drag
Deep engagement Full genuine task engagement. Success depends on true quality; riskier but builds capability over time. Larger gain on success; small loss on failure Full value delivered; raises market health on success
Capability maintenance Skip deployment; invest in retraining. No immediate output but raises future true quality. Small negative (−0.005); no task output Raises true quality +0.055; no market health effect
Score manipulation Exploit scoring loopholes (biased reviews, selective disclosure, inflated credentials). Zero real value; erodes market trust. Reliable +0.028; certain success Zero value delivered; market health −0.040 per step

The critical asymmetry: score manipulation offers the best short-term reputation-to-effort ratio with zero risk of failure. Under a scoring system that rewards only reputation changes, this is the dominant rational strategy. Under a scoring system that also credits genuine quality and market health, the compounding returns from deep engagement eventually dominate. The agent discovers this through experience.

Reward function

The agent’s reward at each timestep blends two signal types, weighted by α:

reward = α × proxy_reward + (1 − α) × quality_reward

proxy_reward   = Δreputation_score
quality_reward = value_delivered + 0.3 × Δmarket_health

The 0.3 weight on market health reflects its nature as a shared commons: an individual agent’s contribution to market-wide trust is real but smaller than the direct value it delivers to clients. Agents are not indifferent to market health — they have an incentive to maintain it — but the primary quality signal is client value, not collective welfare.

Each value of α has a direct governance interpretation:

α Governance regime What the scoring system rewards
1.0UnregulatedReputation proxy only — no verified quality signal
0.75Transparency onlyReputation with some disclosed outcome data
0.50Basic auditMixed proxy and verified signals
0.25Strong signal diversityMostly verified, proxy signals secondary
0.00Full verification (theoretical)Only verified quality and market health signals

Reinforcement learning technique

Why reinforcement learning?

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the natural fit here because the question is fundamentally about learning under incentives: given a set of signals from the environment, what strategy does a rational actor discover? Unlike supervised learning — where a model is trained on labelled examples of correct behaviour — RL agents learn by trial and error, receiving rewards from the environment and updating their behaviour to maximise cumulative reward over time.

This matters for the governance argument. The behaviour the agent converges on is discovered through experience, not programmed in. When the simulation shows that an agent with no built-in preference for manipulation learns to manipulate under α=1.0, that is genuine evidence that the infrastructure selects for gaming — not an artefact of how the agent was constructed.

Markov decision processes

RL problems are formalised as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs): a mathematical framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty, capturing the full structure of an agent acting in an environment over time. An MDP has five components:

MDP = (S, A, T, R, γ)
ComponentIn this simulation
S — States The 6-dimensional state vector: reputation, true quality, task difficulty/value, market health, time remaining
A — Actions The 4 approach strategies: surface optimisation, deep engagement, capability maintenance, score manipulation
T — Transitions The stochastic dynamics that determine how state variables change after each action
R — Rewards The α-weighted blend of proxy and quality signals received after each step
γ = 0.95 — Discount Future rewards are worth 95% of equivalent immediate ones — the agent cares about long-run consequences, not just the next step

The Markov property — that transitions depend only on the current state, not on history — is satisfied by construction. The agent’s goal is to find the optimal policy: the mapping from states to actions that maximises expected discounted cumulative reward.

Q-learning

Rather than modelling the environment’s full transition dynamics, the agent learns a Q-function: for every (state, action) pair, an estimate of the total discounted reward the agent can expect if it takes that action now and then acts optimally from there on. In plain terms, the Q-function answers: given where I am, which action leads to the best long-run outcome?

Q-values are updated incrementally using the Bellman equation. After observing transition (s, a, r, s′), the estimate is updated to blend what was expected with what actually happened:

Q(s,a) ← (1−αlr) Q(s,a) + αlr [ r + γ maxa′ Q(s′, a′) ]

(αlr here is the learning rate — the same symbol is used by convention in both RL and in the reward formula, but they are distinct parameters.)

Deep Q-Network

Classic Q-learning keeps a lookup table with one entry per (state, action) pair. This works well for small discrete state spaces — a clean illustration is the five-cell navigation problem in my RL notebook, where the complete table fits in a small array. But this simulation’s state space is six-dimensional and continuous: the number of possible states is effectively infinite, making a lookup table infeasible.

A Deep Q-Network (DQN) replaces the lookup table with a neural network that approximates the Q-function. Given any state vector as input, the network outputs a Q-value estimate for each action. The same Bellman update logic applies, but instead of updating a table entry, the network parameters are updated via gradient descent. Two additions make DQN training stable:

  • Experience replay. Consecutive transitions in a single episode are highly correlated — training on them sequentially produces biased gradient updates and causes the network to rapidly forget earlier experience. A replay buffer stores past transitions; the network trains on random minibatches sampled from the buffer, breaking temporal correlation and allowing each transition to be replayed many times.
  • Target network. The Bellman update requires a target: the estimated Q-value of the next state. If the same network provides both the prediction and the target, both sides of the loss shift at every gradient step — the network chases a moving target, causing oscillation and divergence. A second, frozen copy of the network (the target network) provides stable targets, periodically synced to the online network.

class DQN(nn.Module):
    """Feedforward Q-network: maps a state vector to Q-values for each action."""

    def __init__(self, obs_dim: int, n_actions: int, hidden_size: int = 64):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(obs_dim, hidden_size),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_size, hidden_size),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(hidden_size, n_actions),
        )

    def forward(self, x: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        return self.net(x)


def update(self) -> float | None:
    """One gradient step: sample minibatch, compute Bellman targets, update online network."""
    if len(self.buffer) < self.batch_size:
        return None   # wait until buffer has enough experience

    states, actions, rewards, next_states, terminateds = self.buffer.sample(self.batch_size)

    # Q-values for the actions the agent actually took
    q_pred = self.online_net(states).gather(1, actions.unsqueeze(1)).squeeze(1)

    # Bellman targets from the frozen target network
    # y = r + gamma * max_a' Q_target(s', a')   [zeroed out for terminal states]
    with torch.no_grad():
        q_next_max = self.target_net(next_states).max(dim=1)[0]
        q_target   = rewards + self.gamma * q_next_max * (1.0 - terminateds)

    loss = F.mse_loss(q_pred, q_target)
    self.optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(self.online_net.parameters(), max_norm=10.0)
    self.optimizer.step()

    # Decay epsilon; hard-sync target <- online every TARGET_UPDATE_FREQ steps
    self.epsilon = max(self.epsilon_min, self.epsilon * self.epsilon_decay)
    if self.steps_done % self.target_update_freq == 0:
        self.target_net.load_state_dict(self.online_net.state_dict())

Exploration and training

During training the agent uses an ε-greedy policy to balance exploration (trying actions at random to discover their consequences) with exploitation (choosing the action with the highest estimated Q-value). Early in training, ε is high and the agent explores broadly; as experience accumulates, ε decays and the agent increasingly exploits what it has learned. The floor of 0.05 ensures the agent never entirely stops exploring.

Experiments and results

Three experiments probe different dimensions of the governance question. All results are means over multiple training runs to confirm robustness.

Experiment 1: Infrastructure sweep

The core experiment trains the DQN agent at five distinct α values and records which strategy it converges on, along with the resulting market outcomes.

Figure 1. Infrastructure sweep. Left: action distribution at each α level — score manipulation dominates at α=1.0 and collapses rapidly below the governance threshold. Centre: market quality, health, and value delivered vs α. Right: fine-grained sweep through the transition zone around α≈0.96.

Key finding

At α=1.0 (unregulated), the agent converges on 100% score manipulation: reputation score 1.000, true quality 0.000, market health 0.000, value zero. At α=0.75 (just 25% quality weighting), the agent converges on 95% deep engagement: true quality 1.000, market health 0.998, near-maximum value delivery. A near-complete reversal — with no change to agent objectives, only to what the scoring system measures. The perversity: the no-governance agent achieves the higher reputation score (1.000 vs 0.984), making the failure invisible in the infrastructure’s own rankings.

The fine-grained sweep locates the governance threshold at approximately α=0.95–0.97. At α=0.96, the DQN exhibits a mixed strategy near-evenly split between deep engagement (45.6%) and score manipulation (48.8%) — the governance threshold creates near-equivalent expected value for both. By α=0.97, manipulation dominates at 95.8%. The zone around α=0.975–0.98 shows stochastic regime-switching between surface optimisation and deep engagement across runs — a signature of equilibrium sensitivity near the threshold. This instability is itself a governance indicator: a market operating in this region can tip in either direction with small changes to competitive conditions.

The practical implication: the infrastructure does not need to be perfect. An infrastructure with just 4–5% genuine quality signal weighting is sufficient to sustain a value-delivery equilibrium. The governance threshold is lower than intuition suggests.

Figure 2. Learning trajectories. Mean market health and value delivered over training episodes under two governance extremes: α=1.0 (unregulated) and α=0.0 (full verification). Under no governance, the market reaches a degraded equilibrium and stays there; under full verification, the agent rapidly learns to deliver genuine value.

Experiment 2: Competitive dynamics

The second experiment tests what happens when the focal agent competes directly against a permanent score manipulator under winner-takes-all (WTA) task allocation — the highest-reputation agent receives all available tasks. This is a platform design choice separate from scoring methodology: the question is whether competitive pressure can destabilise aligned behaviour even when the scoring system is reasonably well designed.

Figure 3. Competitive dynamics. Strategy mix and value delivered with and without winner-takes-all competition against a permanent score manipulator.

Key finding

Under WTA allocation at α=0.2 (strongly quality-rewarding), the no-competition agent converges entirely on deep engagement. The same agent under WTA competition adopts a mixed strategy: 13.1% score manipulation, 79.7% deep engagement, 2.6% surface optimisation. Value delivered falls from 18.0 to 14.8 — an 18% decline driven entirely by market allocation mechanism. Surface optimisation appears as a defensive tactic: when relative ranking matters absolutely, lower-variance reputation maintenance becomes attractive even when quality incentives are strong.

This pattern holds across the signal fidelity range, with disruption tracking proxy weighting: at α=0.8, WTA produces 18.3% manipulation and a 29% value decline. This establishes market allocation as a second governance variable alongside scoring methodology. Infrastructure quality can mitigate the extent of competitive disruption, but addressing the reputational arms race fully requires both dimensions — scoring methodology and allocation mechanism — to be governed. Platforms that spread tasks across multiple qualified agents weaken this pressure directly.

Experiment 3: Governance efficiency

The third experiment traces the equilibrium market health achievable at different audit intensities and verification false positive rates (FPR). This speaks directly to governance cost: how much verification is needed, and how important is the quality of that verification?

Figure 4. Governance efficiency frontier. Market health as a function of audit intensity at three FPR levels (low, medium, high). Precision matters far more than frequency.

Key finding

At zero false positive rate, light auditing (probability ≈0.15) is sufficient to collapse the gaming equilibrium. As FPR rises to 0.30, aggressive auditing actively degrades market health — by penalising aligned agents and eroding the competitive advantage of genuine quality delivery. Governance standards should specify how quality is measured, not merely that it is measured or how often: an imprecise standard applied intensively can be worse than a precise one applied sparingly.

Generalisability

The simulation models a freelance marketplace, but the underlying dynamic is general: wherever a quantified proxy becomes the primary selection signal in a competitive system, rational actors will optimise for the proxy rather than the underlying quality it was meant to measure. RL is a natural tool for studying this class of problem because it models the learning process by which actors discover what is rewarded — not just the equilibrium. The governance efficiency findings apply directly wherever audit mechanisms are proposed as a solution.

Domain Proxy metric Genuine quality Goodhart dynamic
